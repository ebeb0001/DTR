\section{Future directions}
The work presented in this thesis provides a first implementation of a railway digital twin based on real infrastructure 
and operational data. However, several extensions could be considered in order to transform the current simulation 
framework into a more complete decision-support tool for railway operators and researchers. This section presents four 
future research directions that naturally follow from the proposed macro-level and micro-level modeling approaches.

\subsection{Counterfactual Simulation and What-If Scenarios}
A first direction for future work concerns the development of counterfactual simulations and what-if scenarios. 
In the current implementation, the simulation primarily aims to reproduce railway operations based on existing 
infrastructure and, when possible, real train trajectories. A counterfactual extension would go one step further by 
allowing the user to modify specific elements of the system and observe how these modifications affect the simulated 
railway traffic.

In practice, this could consist of testing alternative operational or infrastructure configurations. For example, a user 
could simulate the closure of a station, the removal of a track section, a change in train frequency, the introduction 
of additional services, or the modification of departure times. The objective would not only be to reproduce what 
happened in the real network, but also to evaluate what could have happened under different assumptions.

This direction could be implemented by extending the current scenario generation pipeline. Since the macro-level model 
already represents stations and connections as a graph, it could be used to rapidly test high-level disruptions or 
modifications in station-to-station connectivity. The micro-level model, on the other hand, could support more detailed 
counterfactual experiments, such as the unavailability of a platform, a switch, or a track segment. In both cases, the 
simulation configuration could include a scenario layer defining which infrastructure elements are modified, removed, or 
constrained before the simulation is executed.

The main utility of this extension would be to transform the digital twin into an exploratory tool. Railway operators 
could use it to evaluate the consequences of operational decisions before applying them in the real network. Researchers 
could also use it to compare different traffic management strategies under controlled and reproducible conditions. This 
would make the framework useful not only for simulation, but also for planning, resilience analysis, and decision support.

\subsection{Tracks Works Planning}
A second future direction concerns the use of the digital twin for track works planning. Railway maintenance and 
infrastructure works often require temporary closures, reduced capacity, speed restrictions, or rerouting strategies. 
These interventions are necessary to preserve infrastructure quality, but they can also generate significant operational 
constraints. A simulation-based tool could therefore help evaluate the impact of planned works before they are carried 
out.

In the context of this thesis, this extension would be particularly relevant because the generated network already 
contains infrastructure elements that can be selectively modified. At the macro level, track works could initially be 
represented by removing or penalizing railway connections between stations. This would make it possible to evaluate 
whether trains can still be routed through alternative paths and to estimate the effect of the works on the global 
connectivity of the selected network area.

At the micro level, track works could be modeled with a higher level of detail. Specific track segments, switches, or 
platforms could be marked as unavailable during a given time window. The route generation and simulation process would 
then need to take these constraints into account. This could be implemented by adding temporal availability attributes to 
the infrastructure graph and by adapting the routing algorithm so that it avoids unavailable elements when computing 
train paths.

The main benefit of this direction is operational. A digital twin capable of simulating planned works could help identify 
bottlenecks, anticipate rerouting needs, and compare several maintenance schedules. It could also support the evaluation 
of different intervention strategies, for example by comparing a short but highly disruptive closure with a longer 
intervention affecting fewer services. In this way, the framework could contribute to a better balance between 
infrastructure maintenance and service continuity.

\subsection{Real-Time Forecasting and Incident Propagation}
A third future direction is the integration of real-time forecasting and incident propagation mechanisms. The current 
implementation mainly focuses on reconstructing and simulating railway operations from static or historical datasets. 
However, a railway digital twin becomes more powerful when it can continuously integrate new operational information and 
use it to predict the evolution of the network state.

This extension would consist of using live or near-real-time data to update the simulation state. For example, if a train 
is delayed at a given station, the digital twin could estimate how this delay may propagate to subsequent stations, to 
other trains, or to shared infrastructure sections. The objective would be to move from retrospective simulation toward 
predictive simulation, where the system helps anticipate future disruptions before they fully materialize.

This direction could be implemented progressively. A first step would be to use the existing punctuality data as 
historical input to train statistical or machine learning models capable of estimating delay propagation patterns. These 
models could then be connected to the simulation pipeline. The macro-level model could be used to study propagation at 
the station-to-station level, while the micro-level model could be used to analyse how local infrastructure conflicts 
contribute to the spread of delays. In a more advanced version, the simulation could be updated dynamically when new 
train positions, delays, or incidents are received.

The utility of this extension is particularly important for operational monitoring. A digital twin with forecasting 
capabilities could help identify the stations or track sections most likely to be affected by an ongoing incident. It 
could also support proactive traffic management by testing corrective actions, such as rerouting, rescheduling, or 
temporary prioritization of certain trains. This would make the digital twin not only a representation of the railway 
system, but also a tool for anticipating and mitigating disruptions.

\subsection{Framework Generalization}
A final future direction concerns the generalization of the proposed framework. The implementation developed in this 
thesis is based on Belgian railway data and on the specific structure of the available infrastructure and operational 
datasets. While this makes the work relevant for the considered case study, a more general framework would allow the same 
methodology to be applied to other railway networks, datasets, or simulation contexts.

Generalization would require separating the core modeling logic from the data-specific processing steps. In the current 
implementation, several transformations are adapted to the format and assumptions of the available datasets. A future 
version of the framework could introduce a more modular architecture, where each data source is handled by a dedicated 
adapter. These adapters would transform external datasets into a common internal representation of stations, tracks, 
switches, platforms, and train movements.

Such a modular design would also make it easier to support different levels of detail. Depending on the available data, 
the framework could generate either a macro-level model, a micro-level model, or a hybrid representation combining both. 
This would be useful because railway data availability varies significantly between countries, operators, and 
infrastructure managers. Some networks may provide detailed track-level data, while others may only provide station-level 
connectivity and timetable information.

The main utility of framework generalization would be to increase the scientific and practical value of the work. From a 
research perspective, it would make the approach easier to reproduce and compare across different railway networks. From 
an operational perspective, it would allow the framework to be adapted to new contexts without rewriting the entire 
pipeline. Ultimately, this would move the project from a case-specific implementation toward a reusable methodology for 
railway digital twin generation and simulation.
